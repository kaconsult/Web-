The Middle Corridor: what it means for investors and traders

Freight between China and Europe through the Caucasus has grown roughly eightfold since before 2022. The infrastructure has not kept up, and that gap is where the opportunity lies.

The route

The Trans-Caspian International Transport Route runs by rail from western China across Kazakhstan, then by ferry over the Caspian Sea from Aktau and Kuryk to Baku and Alat. From there it crosses Azerbaijan and Georgia by rail and reaches Europe either through the Black Sea ports of Poti and Batumi or overland into Türkiye on the Baku–Tbilisi–Kars railway. It is the shortest route between western China and Europe that avoids Russia.

The numbers

~0.5m tAnnual volume before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine
4.1m tVolume in 2024, up 63% on 2023
+22%Growth in Jan–Aug 2026; East–West transit up 55%

According to Kazakhstan's Ministry of Transport, growth has continued into 2026. In its 2023 study, the World Bank estimated that with the right reforms and investment, volumes could triple and travel times could halve by 2030.

The bottlenecks

The corridor is still small. It handles about 6% of the 100 million tonnes a year that the Northern route through Russia can carry. Georgian port capacity is close to its limit, and funding for the Anaklia deep-sea port has been cut. The Caspian Sea has been falling by up to 30 cm a year since 2020, which limits how much the ferries can load. There is no single operator or unified tariff, and every border crossing and transshipment adds time.

Where the opportunity is

  • Trade finance. Goods moving along the route need pre-export finance, bank credit lines and letters of credit. In our experience, mid-sized traders often struggle to get bank credit on workable terms.
  • Logistics real estate. As volumes grow, so does demand for modern warehousing around the main hubs: Tbilisi, Poti and Batumi, Baku and Alat, and Aktau.
  • Banking and payments. Companies trading across four jurisdictions need accounts and payment flows that satisfy the compliance teams of banks in every country on the route.

The risks

The corridor depends on stability in the South Caucasus and on the wider region, including Iran. Infrastructure projects run late, and customs and regulatory rules differ from country to country. Analysts at the Carnegie Endowment describe the corridor as a window of opportunity rather than a permanent pathway to European markets. Deals here need to be structured with that in mind.
